# Linking the climate table to published papers: first results

*For the one-on-one of 9 October 2026. About two pages.*

## 1. What we built from the table

We picked three small, fixed pieces of the table to test against the literature. All three compare the table's modelled historical decade (1995–2004) with its end-of-century decade (2085–2094) under the high-emissions scenario RCP8.5, and in all three the value goes up:

- **One grid cell (R106C361):** the summer Fire Weather Index, a weather-based fire-danger rating, rises from about 25.1 to 31.2.
- **The ten cells labelled Oklahoma / Stephens:** the same summer Fire Weather Index rises by about 6.1 on average across the ten.
- **The 2,827 cells labelled California (with a value):** the number of summer days with a heat index above 105 °F rises by about 13 days on average per cell.

**Caveats that still stand:** the county and state groupings, the meaning of several fields, and the averaging are our reading of the table. None of it has been confirmed by you or the data owner.

## 2. What we did with the papers

- **The collection.** Our colleague collected 1,918 papers with a search on hazard words (heat, fire, drought, flood and so on).
- **A random check.** We drew 10 papers at random and read each one twice. The second reader worked independently, without seeing our units or the first reading. Between them the two readers recorded 48 statements. **None mentioned the Fire Weather Index or the heat index, none named a US place, and none named an emissions scenario.** So a random sample of this collection will almost never touch our units, and searching on purpose is justified.
- **A targeted search.** We looked for papers that contain, as whole words, both one of our concept names ("fire weather index", "FWI", "heat index") and one of our place names ("Oklahoma", "Stephens", "California"). Exactly **4** of the 1,918 papers qualified. We fixed that list before opening any of them.
- **A double reading of the four.** We read the 4 papers twice independently in the same way and settled disagreements by fixed rules that keep the more cautious value. This gave 26 statements:
  - 6 recorded the same way by both readers ("confirmed by a second independent reading");
  - 7 found by both readers with small differences, settled conservatively;
  - 13 found by only one reader.

## 3. What the four papers turned out to be

- **A global computer model of fires** (vegetation, ignition, burnt area, emissions, worldwide). It names the Fire Weather Index only in a list of other tools. Its one "place" match, **"Stephens", is an author's surname** in a citation.
- **A daily fire-hazard index for Sardinia, Italy.** Its background section reviews the Fire Weather Index at some length, but its own results are about a different index. California appears only as the place where an older method was once tested.
- **The 2017–18 wet-then-dry swing and the spring 2018 wildfires in Oklahoma and Texas.** This is a genuinely relevant paper: wildfires, acres burned, the Rhea Fire in Dewey County, Oklahoma. But **its "FWI" is a soil-moisture "fractional water index"**, not the Fire Weather Index. It qualified for our search on a coincidence of letters.
- **Summer heat at US prisons, 1990–2023.** California prisons are among the most heat-exposed. The paper measures air temperature. It says (citing another study) that the heat index did not describe the health risk any better, so it does not use it.

## 4. The link test

For every statement against every unit, we compare five things:

- **What** it is about (the concept);
- **where**;
- **when**;
- **under which emissions scenario**;
- **which direction** the change goes.

A pair counts as a candidate link only if the concept matches, the place matches exactly, and nothing contradicts.

| Result over 78 comparisons (26 statements × 3 units) | Count |
| --- | ---: |
| Candidate links | **0** |
| Same concept by name, but place, period and scenario not stated ("related but not enough") | 2 |
| A clear conflict | 3 |
| Cannot be decided (mostly: a different concept, or a place that is not exactly ours) | 73 |

**The two "related" pairs** are one statement from the Sardinia paper, found by only one reader: comparison studies found the Fire Weather Index performed better than other methods. It names our concept against both fire units, but no place, no period and no scenario.

**The three conflicts** are one prison statement: 67% of prisons had at least one day with a daily mean above 85 °F in 2020–2023. It is a recent observation-period finding, compared with our end-of-century units.

The near misses were on place:
- Dewey County, Oklahoma is not Stephens County.
- "California, Arizona and Nevada" listed together is not California alone under our exact-match rule.
- The Oklahoma and Texas panhandles are inferred from the paper, not stated with the claim.

## 5. What this shows

- **No link exists yet** between these four papers and our three units under our rules.
- **Matches only come from searching on purpose.** Random reading produced nothing near our units. Targeted search produced papers that are related, but still not about the same thing at the same place.
- **The emissions scenario is the universal gap.** None of the 74 statements we have recorded across both rounds of reading (48 + 26) names one, so even a perfect concept-and-place match could only ever be a qualified link.
- **"Heat index" in a paper is not "days above 105 °F".** "FWI" in a paper is not even always the Fire Weather Index. Matching on words alone is not enough, which is why every match is read and checked by two readers.
